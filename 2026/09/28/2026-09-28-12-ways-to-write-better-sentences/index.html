12 Ways to Write Better Sentences for Creative Writers

Created2026-09-28|Updated2026-09-28|英语乐园
|Post Views:

Summary

Novel editor Ellen Brock presents twelve ways to improve sentences in creative writing. The notes cover clarity, tone, active voice, filtering, pace, natural wording, and unnecessary words, with examples, writing terminology, and a Chinese translation.

Content

以下为原笔记中的视频转录与学习内容，保留原素材的叙述语境。

Hey guys, it’s Ellen Brock, novel editor. I hope you’re all doing really, really well today.

Today we’re going to talk about 12 ways to write better sentences as a creative writer. I’m also working on a video of ways to write better paragraphs that will probably come out in the new year, so stay tuned for that if you think that would help you. Today, my goal with this video is to really help your writing to sound more professional—more like a published author. There are some tips and tricks that can really help to give your writing that extra edge, that extra sense of professionalism, which will be really helpful when you go to query publishers or agents, or wherever you’re trying to get your story seen.

There may always be reasons why, for you in particular, you might want to ignore a tip that I give in this video, and that’s completely fine. There are a wide variety of writing styles, and sometimes you might go against a certain tip because you want to create a certain effect, and that’s totally fine. But for the most part, my tips in this video should apply no matter what point of view, style, or genre you’re writing in. These are really just ways to give your writing a little bit of a professional polish.

So with that said, let’s go ahead and get right into the tips.

Number 1: Avoid confusingly complex sentences.

Longer, more complicated sentences might be something that you do choose to do for a stylistic effect. It might also be something that you want to use to break up monotony, so you want to make your sentences have different lengths and structures so that it doesn’t become repetitive or monotonous in sound. However, when you use overly complicated sentences unnecessarily or too often, it actually slows down the reading process because the reader has to try a lot harder to understand the meaning of that sentence—what you’re trying to say, how it’s relevant—and that can make the writing itself feel too slow. It can also make the actions feel more distant because you start to layer concepts and cram concepts into the same sentence.

I’m going to give an example to hopefully clarify what I mean:

“Because Jessica was hungry due to missing breakfast, which happened because Jake, her brother, had taken too long to get ready in their shared bedroom, she was forced to once again circle around the large block looking for the food truck that always seemed to be in a different place each day.”

This sentence is grammatically correct. It’s pretty understandable in isolation and doesn’t really pose any particular problem. But if you were to take a sentence like this and combine it with several other lengthy, complicated sentences—either right up against each other or even just many times on a page or in a chapter—it will really slow down the process of reading. The reader will have to spend longer understanding what you’re saying, it may bury the point you’re trying to make, and it may also create distance from the action.

So normally, complicated sentences like this are better off broken up into smaller sentences. For example, we could rewrite this sentence:

“Jessica’s stomach rumbled. If Jake hadn’t taken so long to get ready in their shared bathroom, she would have had time to eat breakfast. Her heels clacked against the sidewalk as she walked around the block looking for the food truck. Why did they park the thing in a different place every day?”

Notice how breaking up this sentence, as a bonus, also gives us more room for sensory details, for some personality and introspection, and it really helps to create a more immersive experience for the reader.

If you have trouble identifying when sentences are getting kind of complicated or lengthy, someone on my Discord recommended the Hemingway Editor. If you put this sentence into the Hemingway Editor, it has no problem identifying that the original version is very hard to read and that the edited version is easy to read. So that’s something that you could use if you do struggle to tell when a sentence is getting too complicated. The Hemingway Editor actually can help with several of the things that we’re going to talk about in this video, so that’s just a little tip that might help you.

Number 2: Set the tone with word choices.

Often when we’re writing, we can choose from a variety of words that mean basically the same thing as what we’re trying to say, but different words have very different connotations and evoke different kinds of moods. By choosing the right word, you can really improve the overall tone and vibe of your writing.

For example:

  • “The boat sliced through the water” feels aggressive or strong.
  • “The boat bobbed across the water” feels lazy or slow.
  • “The boat floated over the water” might feel hopeful or even uplifting.

This also goes for similes. If you’re writing a romantic scene, you don’t want to describe the roses like blood spots on the bed. And if you’re writing a horror, you don’t want to describe the long hallway as a giraffe’s neck because it just doesn’t set the right mood and tone. So be thoughtful about the words that you’re choosing and the impact that’s having on the vibe that your writing is creating.

Let’s look at an example from the novel The Ritual by Adam Nevill. This is, of course, a horror novel, and pay attention to the words that he chooses:

“He was interrupted by a crash out there somewhere in the length and breadth of the countless trees, in the ocean of invisible ruin and tangle. A great bough or strong limb had been snapped in half. The sounds of its breaking seemed to shoot in so many directions it became impossible to guess where the sound originated.”

Notice how the word choices emphasize the largeness of the woods with “the ocean of invisible ruin and tangle.” Also notice the emphasis on more aggressive-sounding words like “snapped in half” rather than “broke,” and the sound “shoots” in so many directions rather than “bounces” or “echoes.” Try to create the mood or the vibe that you want the reader to experience with your word choices, and this will really go a long way towards improving your writing.

Number 3: Avoid passive voice.

You’ve probably heard a million times that you should avoid passive voice. Sometimes it can be a little tricky to understand what that means, but to make it as simple as possible: passive voice is when the subject of a sentence or the clause of a sentence receives the action instead of performs the action. So if you’re struggling to tell if it is passive voice or not, just try to ask yourself: “Is the subject doing the action? Are they performing the action?”

For example, “The door was held by Henry” is passive because the door is not doing the action, whereas “Henry held the door” is active. Henry is the one doing the action of holding the door. Often in fiction, this can be a problem because it results in de-emphasizing the character doing the action.

Here’s another example: “Megan was chased by Elizabeth” is weak compared to “Elizabeth chased Megan.” In the first sentence, Megan is receiving the action, and then at the end… in the second sentence, Elizabeth is doing the action. Even if Megan is the protagonist (the character we generally want to emphasize more), the writing will feel stronger if we focus on the person doing the action. If we do want to put more emphasis on Megan, another option might be “Megan ran from Elizabeth,” which puts the emphasis on Megan and allows Megan to still be the one who’s actually doing the action—so we’re still not using passive voice.

There may, of course, be situations where we want to use passive voice on purpose to create a certain effect, such as intentionally creating distance. A character might do this for some reason, such as not wanting to take responsibility or maybe feeling guilty. So in deep third-person POV or in first-person POV, it’s possible that passive voice might help to convey that intentional distancing from the action, but I would say that that type of situation is relatively rare. If you do need help identifying passive voice, again, the Hemingway Editor can really help with that; it does a pretty good job of identifying passive voice.

Number 4: Remove filtering.

So, filtering is when the experience of the story is filtered through the POV (point of view) character, so that character’s experience is emphasized as separate from the reader. That’s not really what the intention is; that’s just the effect of filtering. Filtering typically involves using words like thought, knew, felt, saw, smelled, tasted—things that create a separation from the sensory experience and the reader.

I’m going to give examples because I think that makes this a lot easier to understand. A common filtering word in amateur fiction is saw. For example: “Rebecca saw the tail of a fox sticking out from the brush.” It’s more distancing than “The tail of a fox stuck out from the brush.”

Of course, there are sensory words where there isn’t really any way to work around them, for example, smelled and tasted. It’s not really easy or feasible to try to write a sentence where you describe that without using the word smelled or tasted, but we can still create a slightly more immersive effect by, again, eliminating filtering that through the point of view character. For example: “Inside the house, Jake smelled green tea and baked bread” could be easily made stronger by rewriting it as “The house smelled of green tea and baked bread,” which eliminates that distance between the sensory experience and the reader.

Introspection can also be less impactful and more distancing when we use filtering like knew, wondered, thought, and things along those lines. Whenever we’re using a deep point of view or first-person point of view, we can assume that it’s the character (the POV character) thinking the thought or having the realization. So those words just add a little bit of extra and unnecessary separation between the reader and that thought or realization. For example: “James thought it was impossible that Katie didn’t know that already” is a lot weaker than “It was impossible for Katie not to know that already.” We don’t need to say that James thought it was impossible because James is the point of view character, so we can simply say “It was impossible,” and it doesn’t make the writing feel quite as distant.

Of course, there are times where filtering might help with clarity, such as an omniscient point of view where there’s a difference between what the narrator knows and experiences versus what the characters know and experience. So in that case, filtering might help with clarity. But for most writers today, the majority are writing in deep third limited or first person, and in neither of those cases is filtering really beneficial.

Let’s look at an example from The Ritual. At the beginning of this excerpt, it’s pointed out that a character is watching something, which is a form of filtering. However, in this case, the character is exhausted and inactive, so it may be intended to put emphasis on this inaction by saying that he watched what’s occurring. But note how the rest of the paragraph has no filtering and a lot of sensory details:

“Luke watched. He pushed the last leg of the stool through the door of the iron stove they were huddled around. A shower of sparks erupted inside as he rooted the ancient wooden spoke around the hearth. They coughed in the acrid smoke that belched out; the chimney was almost totally closed. Smoldering remnants of the seat formed the base to the red ashes inside the little oven that only heated part of the ground floor before the drafts from the door and between the floorboards took over with a night chill, a tang of damp earth, and the ferment of rotten wood.”

In that excerpt, we get a lot of sensory information—sights and smells, even a little bit of taste maybe, of the smoke—and we did not need to use filtering words to bring that across, so it’s a much more immersive experience for the reader. So if possible, eliminating filtering is a great way to make the reading experience feel a lot more immediate, a lot more connected to the reader.

Number 5: Set the pace with sentence length.

It’s not only word choices that can help with setting the tone or the mood; the sentence length can also go a long way towards setting the tone or mood of a scene.

For example, in most cases, if the character is being thoughtful or maybe they’re distant from something that occurred, longer sentences tend to work better. They give a more introspective, thoughtful mood. Whereas if the character is involved in an intense or immediate emotional situation—action, urgency, all of that—it is better conveyed by shorter, choppier sentences. This is a really great way to control the pace that the reader reads.

When we have shorter, choppier sentences, we tend to read faster, and that can help to give a sense of tension and urgency. Whereas when you want to give that more creative, thoughtful, introspective type of vibe—maybe the character is observing things, they’re thinking about the way things look—the descriptions (visual descriptions, sensory descriptions), those things tend to work better in longer form with longer sentences. Slower reading time equals more of an experiential vibe.

Let’s look at two examples from the same book. We’re going to look at We Need to Talk About Kevin by Lionel Shriver. Notice in this intense scene, where the protagonist comes home to a concerningly quiet house, how short the sentences are:

“As I advanced down the hall, I paused at Celia’s bedroom and had to force myself to walk in. It was dark. Her bed was empty. The same in the master bedroom. The bathrooms. Out on the deck. Nothing. Nobody. Where were you? Had you gone looking for me? I had a mobile; you knew the number. And why wouldn’t you have taken the truck? Was this a game? Were you hiding, giggling in a closet with Celia? This, of all nights, you chose to play a game?”

The shortness conveys her sense of panic and urgency. We can compare this to another point in the book that focuses more on reflection or thoughtfulness:

“Before you condemn me utterly, I beg you to understand just how hard I’d been trying to be a good mother. But trying to be a good mother may be as distant from being a good mother as trying to have a good time is from truly having one. Distrusting my every impulse from the instant he was laid on my breast, I’d followed a devout regime of hugging my little boy an average of three times a day, admiring something he did or said at least twice, and reciting ‘I love you, kiddo’ or ‘You know that your daddy and I love you very much’ with the predictable uniformity of liturgical professions of faith.”

The longer sentences here convey a sense of thoughtfulness as well as emotional distance from the material being discussed. She’s explaining here and wants to be understood, but there isn’t an urgency, and the emotions of the situation feel at least partly past. But if the sentences were shorter or choppier, it would instead come across as more desperate or panicked, or maybe that she has an immense stake in being understood in this exact moment.

The sentence length can vary a lot throughout the novel, even throughout individual scenes. It’s a great way to control the pacing, and it can really give your writing that extra edge.

Number 6: Avoid body parts taking action.

This is a very common habit that amateur writers can get into, but it’s very easy to fix. So once you’re aware of it, you can easily cut it out of your writing.

Sometimes the way sentences are written, the body part of a character is given the action rather than the character themselves doing the action. For example:

  • “His foot kicked the ball” gives the action to the foot, while “He kicked the ball” gives the action to the person.
  • “Her hand grabbed the pen” gives the action to her hand, while “She grabbed the pen” gives the action to the person.

This again creates unnecessary distance from the action, but it’s very simple and easy to take out of your writing. It’s definitely something I recommend to give your writing a little more strength.

Number 7: Reduce “was -ing” construction.

This is another super easy one once you’re aware, and you can easily remove it from your writing. That is just the “was -ing” construction. Was running, was dancing, was jumping feels more distant from the reader and also just feels weaker.

For example:

  • “He was running down the street” could be easily made stronger by rewriting it as “He ran down the street.”
  • “She was jumping up and down” is better as “She jumped up and down.”
  • “He was sitting on the couch thinking about what he would do” is not as strong as “He sat on the couch and thought about what he would do.”

Of course, there might be situations where the “was -ing” construction just sounds more natural or does convey a slightly different meaning, and in that case, it’s completely fine. We should always go with what sounds best and most natural. For example, in dialogue, if a character asks another character, “Where were you at 3 p.m. yesterday?” they will most likely respond with, “I was driving home from work,” rather than, “At 3 p.m. yesterday, I drove home from work.” With all of these tips, the point is to make the writing feel stronger, so if one of these tips ever makes your writing feel more unnatural or awkward, of course, don’t follow it.

Number 8: Keep the wording natural.

Often, writers might go for more unnatural phrasing in an attempt to sound more sophisticated, old-fashioned, or “writerly.” But normally, this doesn’t enhance the writing; it can feel a little bit awkward or even maybe stiff.

For example: “He placed the book upon the dresser” rather than “He set the book on the dresser.” The second sentence is a lot more natural, a lot more how modern people would talk or explain something. The more sophisticated, old-fashioned “He placed the book upon the dresser” sounds kind of awkward to modern readers.

Sometimes this could also take the form of avoiding contractions, such as writing, “He did not know what he should do” rather than “He didn’t know what to do.” There might, of course, be situations where it makes sense for the personality of your character, or maybe the narrator, or the style of writing—there may be reasons why going for a more old-fashioned or sophisticated sound makes sense or works for you.

But for the majority of writers, this is just going to seem odd and unnatural, and it’s going to create distance because of that unnatural sound. That’s going to make it seem less immediate, less close to the reader. So for most people, it is better to just use the more natural-sounding sentence rather than trying to sound sophisticated or writerly. Sometimes this can happen because writers read older books more often, so that’s what books sound like to them. It might also happen if you come from an academic writing background or a scientific writing background.

Number 9: Cut unnecessary similes.

So, similes (or comparisons using like or as) do have a place in writing. I don’t want to suggest that you cannot use them at all; they actually can be used very effectively. But often, amateur writers or new writers might use similes excessively, or in situations where a simile just doesn’t add anything or doesn’t make sense.

Normally, similes for common or well-understood concepts, descriptions, and things like that just don’t really serve any purpose. For example: “He stomped the snow off his shoes like a drummer beating a drum.” This doesn’t really offer any meaningful additional information because we all know what it sounds like and looks like to stomp snow from our shoes.

Additionally, similes don’t work well when the comparison is too similar to the original thing. With a simile, we’re trying to add to or enhance the reader’s understanding of the description. For example: “He ran across the yard like a soccer player running for the ball.” This doesn’t really add new information because it’s just a different way to say that the character is running. We’re not looking to just say the same thing in a different way.

Similes should seek to enhance the reader’s understanding of something that might be unfamiliar or something difficult to grasp, or it should try to set the mood or the tone in a way that might not be obvious inherently with just a simple description. For example, let’s look at this simile from The Handmaid’s Tale by Margaret Atwood:

“The tulips are red, a darker crimson towards the stem, as if they have been cut and are beginning to heal there.”

This creates a clearly dark tone, whereas just describing the tulips on their own as darker right at the stem doesn’t have any particular connotation when it comes to mood, tone, or vibe.

Here’s another example:

“Without his uniform he looks smaller, older, like something being dried.”

This makes the character being described feel clearly unappealing, which adds a layer of additional meaning. Let’s look at one last example from The Ritual:

“Then he realized he could feel nothing, nothing at all, as if every nerve had been stripped from out of his body, like wiring torn from a wall cavity.”

The simile emphasizes the horror element and makes the character’s experience more evocative and more visceral. Similes can be a great way to enhance the mood or tone, or to help the reader grasp something that’s difficult to understand. Just make sure that you’re not including similes just for the heck of it when they’re not really contributing anything to the novel.

Number 10: Use vocabulary level strategically.

Vocabulary level can be a great way to convey characterization, values, education level, and all sorts of different things, but that’s not really what I want to talk about in this video. What I want you to focus on with vocabulary level is the effect that it has on reading speed.

When you use more advanced vocabulary—bigger words, even if you use bigger words that are fairly common and not super obscure—it really slows down the reader. This isn’t necessarily a bad thing; it’s just something to be aware of. Some books are meant to be read more slowly. Some genres, some target reader groups like to read things slowly, whereas other groups prefer something faster-paced. Other genres do better with a faster, more clipped pacing, which can be better achieved with a lower vocabulary level. So pay attention to how your word choices, in terms of the complexity of the words and the advancedness of the words, can impact how slowly or quickly the reader gets through the text.

Let’s look at an example from We Need to Talk About Kevin. This is a good example of more advanced vocabulary being used to convey character in a novel, but also, in my opinion, this book is best read slowly, and the more advanced vocabulary helps to slow the reader down and really experience the story alongside the protagonist. In this excerpt, the protagonist is describing the difference between her troubled son Kevin’s and her daughter Celia’s infancies:

“Kevin’s disconsolate infancy had segued to wholesale boredom. Celia was entranced by the least bauble, every bit as delighted with a scrap of colored tissue paper as with that expensive mother-of-pearl mobile over her crib. She displayed an indiscriminate fascination with a tactile universe that would have driven your Madison Avenue masters to distraction.”

We can compare this to a book that, again in my opinion, is a bit of a quicker read, yet with no less personality or characterization in the writing. This is an excerpt from Survivor by Chuck Palahniuk. From this example, we can really tell that a simpler vocabulary level doesn’t mean that you can’t make interesting points or have a unique style:

“Next to the speakerphone is a fat daily planner book they keep full of things for me to get done. They want me to account for the next ten years, task by task, their way. Everything in your life turns into an item on a list, something to accomplish. You get to see how your life looks flattened out. The shortest distance between two points is a timeline, a schedule, a map of your time, the itinerary for the rest of your life. Nothing shows you the straight line from here to death like a list.”

The excerpt from Survivor is written at a third-grade reading level, and the excerpt from We Need to Talk About Kevin is written at a ninth-grade reading level. This is a really big difference in reading level, but both books feel mature, they both feel interesting, and they both have a lot of characterization. So there’s no sense that it’s less sophisticated to write at a lower reading level. What it does do is it makes the book read a lot faster.

I think, for me, I read Survivor in probably two or three days, whereas I read We Need to Talk About Kevin over probably a month or a month and a half. It just was a book that, in my opinion, felt like it needed to be read more slowly. Think about the vocabulary words you’re choosing and how they’re impacting the pacing of your book. Think about the story and what it calls for—how quickly you want it to be read, how quickly it should be read. The vocabulary level will guide the reader on how long to spend reading the book.

Number 11: Avoid starting actions.

This is another super, super easy one. Having characters “start” or “begin” actions reduces the immediacy of the action and almost never enhances understanding.

For example: “Elizabeth started to laugh” and “Elizabeth laughed” will convey the same meaning in the vast majority of circumstances, but “Elizabeth laughed” feels much stronger and more immediate. For the same reasons, “Mark lunged forward and punched John” is stronger than “Mark lunged forward and began to punch John.” Again, if you have a situation where it does change the meaning to use “start” or “begin,” then by all means do so, but in the majority of cases, this can just be a bad habit that writers get into, and cutting out “start” and “began” from your writing will make it stronger.

Number 12: Cut out unnecessary words.

Wordiness can, of course, be a stylistic choice, but I’m not really talking about wordiness as a style choice here, but rather just using an excessive amount of words that don’t add or enhance meaning. This can create unnecessary distance from the action, and it can also bury the point that you’re trying to make or just create a sort of cluttered sense to your writing where it just doesn’t feel crisp and clean.

Let’s look at this example, which depicts very clearly a common reason for overwriting, which is constantly describing something as being of something else:

“Charles picked up the ax off the stump of the tree, squeezed the handle of the ax, and dug the heel of his shoe into the ground beneath him.”

This could easily be cut down to:

“Charles picked up the ax from the stump, squeezed the handle, and dug his heel into the ground.”

We don’t need “stump of the tree” because we know the stump is from a tree. We don’t need “heel of his shoe” because we can assume Charles is wearing shoes. We don’t need “handle of the ax” because we know this is talking about the ax.

Besides “of” being a common indicator of extra words, “the ground beneath him” is also unnecessary—we already know the ground is beneath him. Unnecessary directional or location-based descriptions can also really bloat writing. For example, if we were to write something like, “Charles looked into the sky above him,” we already know the sky is above him, so it’s an unnecessary amount of extra words.

This might not sound like a huge deal, and in isolation, it really isn’t a big deal. But over the course of an entire novel, this can add up to tons and tons of extra words that just create a lot of distance and clutteredness in the writing. Keep in mind, though, that sometimes you might use extra words either to control the flow or pacing of a sentence to make it sound better, or we might add extra words when there’s a detail that we want to include. For example, we might want to describe Charles’s shoe in this excerpt, so in that case, we might say, “He dug the heel of his boot into the ground,” because we want to make sure that the reader knows that he’s wearing boots rather than just regular shoes. So there may be reasons why you want to do this, but often these are good signs that there are just a lot of unnecessary words that could be cut down.

Vocabulary & Expressions

这段文本是典型的美式口语化教学风格（Creative Writing Pedagogy），使用了大量出版界和创意写作圈的专业行话（Jargon）。以下是关键表达的深度拆解：

1. 写作状态与行业术语

  • Creative writer (创意写作者/文学创作者)：区别于学术写作（academic writing）或商业写作（copywriting），特指写小说、故事、诗歌的人。
  • Query publishers or agents (向出版商或经纪人投递稿件/寻底)：Query 在出版界是一个专有名词，指写“引子信/自荐信”（Query Letter）去说服经纪人或出版商看你的小说大纲和样章。
  • Point of view / POV (视角)：小说写作的核心概念。文本中提到了 Deep third limited (深层第三人称限制视角) 和 First person (第一人称)。Deep POV 指的是消除了叙述者的存在，让读者完全沉浸在角色的感官中。
  • Give your writing a professional polish (给你的文字进行专业润色)：Polish（抛光/润色）是修改稿件的最后阶段，指让句子变流畅、高级。
  • Show up in the new year / Stay tuned (新的一年推出 / 敬请期待)：自媒体视频博主常用转场和预告语。

2. 核心写作技巧名词（高价值干货词汇）

  • Avoid confusingly complex sentences (避免令人困惑的复合句/臃肿句)：指那些嵌套了太多定语从句、状语从句，导致核心信息被埋没的句子。
  • Connotations (字面以外的隐含意义/潜台词)：文字自带的色彩。如博主举例：sliced（切开）带有攻击性，而 bobbed（上下起伏）带有闲适感。
  • Passive voice (被动语态)：英语写作大忌。博主给出了清晰定义：主语接受动作而非发出动作（The subject receives the action instead of performs it）。
  • Filtering / Filtering words (过滤词/视角的抽离)：创意写作极其重要的概念。指写作者非要在感官描写前加上“他看到”、“她感到”、“他听见”。这会在读者和故事场景之间筑起一面墙（Create a separation/distance）。
  • Introspection (内心独白/心理活动)：角色在小说中的自我思考。
  • Immersive experience (沉浸式体验)：现代小说（尤其是类型小说）追求的最高境界，让读者觉得不是在“读”故事，而是“身处”故事之中。
  • Was -ing construction (过去进行时结构/弱动作结构)：如 was running。在创意写作中，除非强调动作正在被中断，否则一律推荐直接用过去式 ran，因为后者更干脆有力（Immediate/Strong）。
  • Body parts taking action (器官自己做动作)：业余作者常犯的错误。例如“他的眼睛看着窗外”（His eyes looked…），正确的应该是“他看着窗外”（He looked…）。
  • Starting actions (启动式动作)：指滥用 start to 或 begin to（开始做某事）。如 started to laugh 不如 laughed 具有冲击力。

3. 地道口语短语

  • Break up monotony (打破单调/打破沉闷)：指交替使用长短句，让行文有节奏感。
  • Cram concepts into the same sentence (把一堆概念硬塞进一个句子里)：极其生动的表达。
  • For the heck of it (随随便便/无缘无故)：口语，意为“只是为了好玩”或“毫无目的地做某事”。
  • Bury the point (埋没重点)：指修饰词太多，导致句子真正的核心意思看不到了。
  • Go a long way towards… (对……大有裨益 / 起到很大作用)。
  • A bit of a quicker read (读起来更轻快/更好读的书)。

中文翻译

大家好，我是小说编辑艾伦·布洛克。希望大家今天过得都非常非常开心！

今天我们来聊聊创意写作者提升句子质量的 12 个方法。另外，我正在制作一期关于如何写好段落的视频，大概会在新年后推出，如果你觉得有帮助的话，记得保持关注哦。今天这期视频的目标，是帮大家让文字听起来更专业——更像出自已出版作家之手。有一些小窍门和细节能让你的作品脱颖而出，多一份专业感。当你向出版商或经纪人投稿，或者想让自己的故事被更多人看到时，这会非常有用。

当然，由于你个人的写作风格不同，总会有一些时候你想要故意忽略我今天讲的某条建议，这完全没有问题。写作风格千差万别，有时为了营造某种特殊的效果，你确实需要反其道而行之。但总的来说，不管你用的是第几人称、什么风格或写什么题材，今天这些技巧大体都是通用的。它们能为你的文字进行一次“专业级的抛光”。

废话不多说，我们直接进入干货。

1. 避免写令人困惑的复杂长句

为了追求某种文风，你可能会刻意选择写一些更长、更复杂的句子。或者为了打破行文的单调感，你可能想通过错落有致的句长和句式结构，来避免文字读起来重复枯燥。然而，如果你毫无必要或过于频繁地使用臃肿复杂的句子，实际上会拖慢阅读节奏。因为读者必须费很大的劲去理解句意——去琢磨你到底想说什么、这些信息有什么关联——这会让故事显得拖沓，也会拉大读者与动作场面之间的距离，因为你把太多的概念一层层塞进了同一个句子里。

我举个例子，这样大家能听得更明白：

“因为杰西卡错过了早餐而感到饥饿，而这又是由于她的哥哥杰克在他们共用的卧室里花太长时间整理自己所导致的，她被迫再次绕着巨大的街区打转，寻找那辆每天似乎都停在不同位置的餐车。”

这个句子语法完全正确。单独拿出来看也算好理解，没什么大问题。但如果你把这样的句子和其他好几个冗长复杂的句子堆在一起——不管是紧挨着，还是在一页或一章里频繁出现——它就会严重拖慢阅读速度。读者需要花更长的时间去理解你的意思，你真正想表达的重点可能会被埋没，动作的临场感也会被削弱。

所以通常情况下，这种复杂的句子最好拆分成短句。例如，我们可以这样重写：

“杰西卡的肚子咕噜直叫。要不是杰克在共用浴室里磨蹭了那么久，她本有时间吃早饭的。她的鞋跟在人行道上敲出咯哒咯哒的响声，绕着街区寻找那辆餐车。这玩意儿怎么天天换地方停？”

你看，这样拆分句子不仅读起来更顺，还为我们留出了更多容纳感官细节、展现人物个性和内心独白的空间，从而为读者创造出一种更具沉浸感的体验。

如果你很难看出自己的句子什么时候变得太复杂或太冗长，我 Discord 社群里的朋友推荐了“海明威编辑器（Hemingway Editor）”。如果你把刚才的第一版句子放进去，它一眼就能识别出原版“极难阅读”，而修改版则“轻松易读”。如果你在判断句子是否过载时遇到困难，可以用用这个工具。其实海明威编辑器还能帮我们解决今天视频里要讲的好几个问题，算是个实用的小贴士。

2. 用词汇选择来奠定基调

在写作时，我们经常会遇到好几个词都能表达同一个大概意思的情况。但不同的词带有完全不同的潜台词（Connotations），能唤起全然不同的情绪。选对词汇，能让作品的整体基调和氛围（Vibe）大不一样。

比如描写船只：

  • “小船切开水面（The boat sliced through the water）” 会让人感到一种攻击性或力量感。
  • “小船在水面颠簸（The boat bobbed across the water）” 感觉就很慵懒、缓慢。
  • “小船在水上漂浮（The boat floated over the water）” 则可能会带来一种充满希望、甚至令人振奋的感觉。

比喻也是同理。如果你在写一个浪漫的场景，你绝对不会把床上的玫瑰花形容得“像床单上的血斑”；而如果你在写恐怖小说，你也绝对不会把长长的走廊形容得“像长颈鹿的脖子”，因为这完全破坏了应有的氛围。所以，请务必用心思考你选择的每一个词，以及它对你想要营造的文本氛围会产生什么影响。

我们来看看亚当·奈维尔的恐怖小说《仪式（The Ritual）》中的一个片段。这是一部恐怖小说，大家注意他的用词：

“远处不知何处的巨响打断了他。那声音来自无边无际、数之不尽的树丛深处，来自那片由隐形的残垣断壁与枯枝藤蔓交织成的海洋。一根巨大的树干或粗壮的树枝被生生折断（snapped in half）。碎裂声似乎同时射向（shoot）四面八方，让人根本无法捕捉声音的源头。”

注意作者是如何通过 “隐形残垣与藤蔓交织的海洋” 来烘托森林的广袤无垠。同时注意他选用了一些极具攻击性的词汇，比如用 “生生折断（snapped in half）” 而不是普通的“折断（broke）”；声音是向四面八方 “激射而出（shoots）”，而不是“回荡（bounces）”或“共鸣（echoes）”。试着用你的词汇选择去创造你想让读者体会到的情绪或氛围，这会对提升你的文笔大有帮助。

3. 避免使用被动语态

你可能已经听过一万遍“要避免使用被动语态”了。有时候可能有点难理解，但简单来说：被动语态就是句子的主语（或从句的主语）是在承受动作，而不是发出动作。如果你拿不准是不是被动语态，就问自己一个问题：“主语是在主动做这个动作吗？”

比如，“门被亨利扶着（The door was held by Henry）” 就是被动，因为门自己没有做出任何动作；而 “亨利扶着门（Henry held the door）” 就是主动，亨利才是执行“扶”这个动作的人。在小说中，被动语态往往是个问题，因为它会弱化那个真正在做出行动的角色。

再举个例子：“梅根被伊丽莎白追赶（Megan was chased by Elizabeth）”，就远不如 “伊丽莎白追赶梅根（Elizabeth chased Megan）” 有力。在第一句里，梅根在承受动作；而在第二句里，伊丽莎白在发出动作。即便梅根是主角（即我们通常更想凸显的角色），只要我们将焦点放在发出动作的人身上，文字就会更有力量。如果我们非要强调梅根，另一个选择是写：“梅根在伊丽莎白的追赶下拼命逃跑（Megan ran from Elizabeth）”，这样既突出了梅根，又让她成为了动作的发出者——我们依然巧妙地避开了被动语态。

当然，有些情况下我们会故意使用被动语态来达到某种效果，比如刻意制造疏离感。某个角色出于某种原因（比如不想承担责任或者感到内疚），可能会这样表达。所以在深层第三人称视角或第一人称视角中，被动语态有时确实能帮上忙，用来表现这种刻意的心理距离，但我得说这种情况非常罕见。如果你需要找被动语态的错，海明威编辑器同样能帮大忙，它识别被动语态很有一套。

4. 消除“过滤词”

所谓“过滤（Filtering）”，是指故事中的种种体验都要先经过视角角色的“过滤”才传达出来，这会导致角色的体验与读者之间产生一层隔阂。这往往不是作者的本意，但“过滤”带来的实际效果就是如此。过滤通常表现为频繁使用这些词：想（thought）、知道（knew）、觉得（felt）、看到（saw）、闻到（smelled）、尝到（tasted）……这些词会在读者和感官体验之间筑起高墙。

我来举几个例子，这样更好理解。在业余小说中，最常见的过滤词就是“看到（saw）”。例如：“丽贝卡看到狐狸尾巴从灌木丛里露了出来。” 这就比直接写 “一条狐狸尾巴从灌木丛里露了出来” 更有距离感。

当然，有些涉及感官的词是躲不掉的，比如“闻到”和“尝到”。你很难在不写“闻到”或“尝到”的情况下把一句话写明白，但我们依然可以通过不把这种体验“过滤”给特定角色，来创造一种更有沉浸感的效果。比如：“进屋后，杰克闻到了绿茶和烤面包的香味。” 这句话完全可以写得更有力：“屋里弥漫着绿茶和烤面包的香味。” 这样就消除了读者与感官体验之间的距离。

另外，当我们使用 知道、纳闷、想 这类的词时，人物的心理描写（Introspection）也会变得没那么有冲击力，显得富有距离。只要我们使用的是深层第三人称限制视角或第一人称视角，读者默认就知道这就是视角角色当下的想法或领悟。因此，这些词纯粹是在读者和角色的思绪之间加戏，完全没有必要。比如：“詹姆斯觉得（thought）凯蒂不可能还不知道这件事。” 这就远不如直接写 “凯蒂不可能还不知道这件事” 锐利。我们不需要特意交代是“詹姆斯觉得”，因为詹姆斯就是视角角色，直接把事实说出来就好，这样文字读起来才够贴身。

当然，有时候过滤词确实能帮我们理清头绪，比如在全知视角（Omniscient POV）中，叙述者知道并经历的事情，和角色们知道并经历的事情是有分别的。在这种情况下，过滤词有助于分清视线。但对当今的大多数写作者来说，大家写的基本上都是深层第三人称限制视角或第一人称视角，在这两种情况下，过滤词几乎没有任何好处。

我们再来看看《仪式》里的一个片段。这个片段的开头指出某个角色正在“看着（watching）”某事，这其实就是一种过滤。但在这种情况下，角色处于极度疲惫、无法动弹的状态，所以作者可能是想通过强调他的“旁观”来表现这种无能为力。但请注意，接下来的大半个段落完全没有使用任何过滤词，全都是满满的感官细节：

“卢克眼睁睁看着。他把那根断了的长凳腿塞进他们围拢的铁炉门里。当他用那根古老的木轮辐拨弄炉床时，里面爆发出一阵火星。烟道几乎完全堵死了，呛人的浓烟喷涌而出，呛得他们直咳嗽。长凳燃尽的残渣成了小炉子里红炭的底座。这小炉子只能温暖一小块地面，紧接着，从门缝和地板间灌进来的夜风便席卷了一切，带来了冰冷的凉意、潮湿泥土的腥气，以及腐木发酵的味道。”

在这个片段中，我们接收到了大量的感官信息——视觉、嗅觉，甚至可能还有烟尘带来的味觉——而作者完全不需要通过“他闻到”、“他感觉到”这些过滤词来传达。读者的体验因此变得极其身临其境。所以如果可能的话，删掉过滤词是让阅读体验变得更有临场感、更贴近读者的绝佳方式。

5. 用句子长度来掌控节奏

不仅词汇选择能烘托基调或氛围，句子的长短在决定场景的基调或情绪上也起着很大作用。

例如，在大多数情况下，如果角色处于沉思中，或者他们对发生的事情感到抽离、疏远，那么长句子往往效果更好，能传达出一种内省、深沉的基调。相反，如果角色正处于激烈或紧迫的情感冲突、动作、紧急状况中，用短小有力、错落的短句来表达效果会好得多。这是控制读者阅读速度的绝佳手段。

当句子短小急促时，我们的阅读速度往往会加快，这有助于营造一种紧张感和紧迫感。而当你想要展现那种更有创意、更具思辨和内省色彩的氛围时——比如角色正在观察周遭，在思考事物的样貌——此时，视觉或感官描写放在长句中效果更佳。阅读速度变慢，意味着读者是在“体验”这种氛围，而不是单纯获取信息。

让我们来看看出自同一本书的两个例子，来自莱昂内尔·施赖弗的《我们得谈谈凯文（We Need to Talk About Kevin）》。注意看这段充满张力的场景，女主角回到家，面对死一般寂静的房子，句式是多么短小：

“我走向走廊深处。在西莉亚的卧室门前我停下了脚步，不得不强迫自己走进去。里面很黑。她的床是空的。主卧也是一样。浴室里。露台上。什么都没有。一个人都没有。你去哪了？你去找我了吗？我带了手机，你知道号码。你为什么不把皮卡开走？这是个游戏吗？你是不是和西莉亚躲在衣柜里偷笑？偏偏在这天晚上，你选在今晚玩游戏？”

短促的句式将她内心的惊慌失措和急迫感展现得淋漓尽致。我们可以把这段话与书中另一处侧重于反思和内省的情节对比一下：

“在你彻底谴责我之前，我恳求你理解我为了做一个好母亲付出了多么大的努力。但‘努力做一个好母亲’与‘真正成为一个好母亲’之间的距离，可能就像‘努力玩得开心’和‘真正玩得开心’一样遥远。从他被放在我胸口的那一刻起，我就开始怀疑自己的每一个本能。我遵循着一套虔诚的死板程序：平均每天拥抱我的小男孩三次，至少夸奖他做的某件事或说的某句话两次，并且带着像教会信徒宣誓一样的刻板 uniformity，机械地重复着‘我爱你，宝贝’或者‘你知道爸爸妈妈非常爱你’。”

这里的长句子传递出了一种沉思感，同时也在情感上与所讨论的事情拉开了距离。她是在解释，渴望被理解，但这里并没有火烧眉毛的紧迫感，场景中的那种情绪在某种程度上已经成为了过去。但如果这里的句子写得短小急促，就会让人觉得她非常绝望、恐慌，或者急切地想要在这一秒被对方理解。

在整部小说中，甚至在单个场景里，句子长度都可以有很大变化。这是控制节奏的王道，绝对能让你的文字更有质感。

6. 避免让“身体部位”自己做动作

这是业余作者极易陷入的一个常见习惯，但它非常好改。只要你意识到了这一点，就能在写作中轻松把它剔除。

有时候，句子的写法会把动作赋予角色的某个身体部位，而不是角色本人。例如：

  • “他的脚踢了球（His foot kicked the ball）” 把动作交给了脚，而 “他踢了球（He kicked the ball）” 把动作交给了人。
  • “她的手抓住了笔（Her hand grabbed the pen）” 把动作交给了手，而 “她抓住了笔（She grabbed the pen）” 把动作交给了人。

这同样会在无形中拉大读者与动作之间的距离。修改方法非常简单，我强烈建议大家改掉这个毛病，让文字更有张力。

7. 减少“was -ing（过去进行时）”结构

这也是一个只要意识到就极易修改的细节。尽量少写“was running（正在跑）”、“was dancing（正在跳舞）”、“was jumping（正在跳跃）”，因为这会给读者带来距离感，而且显得文风软弱。

比如：

  • “他当时正沿着街道奔跑（He was running down the street）”，完全可以直接改成 “他沿着街道奔跑（He ran down the street）”，这样有力得多。
  • “她当时正上下跳跃（She was jumping up and down）”，不如改成 “她上下跳跃（She jumped up and down）”。
  • “他当时正坐在沙发上，想着自己该做什么（He was sitting on the couch thinking about what he would do）”，不如写成 “他坐在沙发上，思索着对策（He sat on the couch and thought about what he would do）”。

当然，有些时候用“was -ing”听起来更自然，或者表达的意思确实有微妙的不同，那时用它完全没问题。我们永远应该以“听起来最好、最自然”为准。比如在对白中，如果一个角色问另一个人：“昨天下午3点你在哪？” 对方多半会回答：“我当时正开车下班回家呢（I was driving home…）”，而不太可能说：“昨天下午3点，我开车下班回家（I drove home…）”。我们讲所有这些技巧的目的，都是为了让文字更强劲，所以如果某条建议反而让你的句子变得拿腔拿调、别扭走样，那就果断忽略它。

8. 保持语言的自然流利

写作者经常为了让自己听起来更博学、更古典或者更有“作家范儿”，去刻意选择一些很不自然的措辞。但这通常并不能为作品加分，反而会让人觉得有些别扭、甚至僵硬。

例如：英文里写 “He placed the book upon the dresser”，就不如直接写 “He set the book on the dresser（他把书放在了梳妆台上）”。第二种写法语气自然，完全是现代人说话和解释事情的方式。而第一种文绉绉、古板的句式在现代读者眼里会显得很违和。

有时候，这也表现为刻意避免使用缩写，比如非要写 “He did not know what he should do”，而不是简干的 “He didn’t know what to do（他不知道该怎么办）”。当然，也有一些情况，比如为了符合你角色的性格、叙述者的设定，或是特定的语言风格，选择一种更古典、更考究的语调是说得通的。

但对大多数创作者而言，这样写只会显得古怪、做作，并且因为这种不自然的腔调而产生距离感，让文字失去那种直击人心的临场感。所以对绝大多数人来说，与其硬去追求所谓的“高大上”或“作家腔”，不如使用最自然的表达。有时候作者犯这个毛病，是因为他们平时读老书比较多，脑子里对小说的印象就是那个调调；如果你有学术界或科学写作背景，也容易出现这种倾向。

9. 删掉无意义的比喻

比喻（在英文里常用 like 或 as）在写作中当然有一席之地。我绝不是说大家完全不能用比喻，实际上它们可以非常有表现力。但业余作者或写作新手往往会滥用比喻，或者在一些完全没必要、甚至说不通的地方硬套比喻。

通常情况下，针对那些大家都心知肚明、极为普通常见的场景和描写，比喻纯属多此一举。例如：“他把鞋上的雪跺掉，就像鼓手在击鼓一样。” 这根本没有提供任何实质性的新信息，因为大家谁不知道跺掉鞋上的雪是什么声音、什么画面？

此外，如果本体和喻体过于相似，比喻也会失效。我们用比喻，是为了拓宽或加深读者对描写的理解。例如：“他穿过院子跑过去，就像足球运动员奔向足球一样。” 这完全没提供任何新意，只不过是把“他在跑步”换了种说法而已。我们写作可不是为了把车轱辘话翻来覆去地讲。

比喻应当致力于帮助读者去理解一些他们感到陌生、或者难以捉摸的事物；或者，去渲染一种仅凭平铺直叙很难表达的情绪或基调。比如，我们来看看玛格丽特·阿特伍德在《使女的故事（The Handmaid’s Tale）》里用的这个比喻：

“郁金香是红色的，越往茎部颜色越深，仿佛它们曾被切开，如今正在那里开始愈合。”

这就精准地营造出了一种阴暗、压抑的基调。如果只是平淡地描写“郁金香接近茎部的颜色比较深”，就不会带有任何特定情绪或氛围的暗示。

再看一个例子：

“脱下制服后，他显得更小、更老了，就像脱了水正在干瘪缩小的东西。”

这立刻让被描写的角色显得非常没有吸引力，平添了一层文本深度。最后看一个《仪式》里的例子：

“接着他意识到自己什么也感觉不到了，彻彻底底麻木了，仿佛全身上下的每一根神经都被抽干了，就像墙腔里被扯断的电线。”

这个比喻极大地强化了恐怖元素，让角色的痛苦体验显得尤为生动、触目惊心。比喻是烘托氛围、帮助读者捕捉抽象概念的绝佳利器。只要确保你不是为了“炫技”而硬塞比喻，确保它们真的对小说有贡献就行。

10. 策略性地运用词汇深度

词汇的高级程度可以很好地用来展示人物性格、价值观、受教育水平等方方面面，但这不是我今天视频想讲的核心。我希望大家把注意力放在词汇难度对读者阅读速度的影响上。

当你使用更高级的词汇——也就是那些更长、更大的词，哪怕它们其实挺常见并不算晦涩——也会明显拖慢读者的阅读速度。这并不一定是坏事，但你心里得有数。有些书原本就是需要慢读的。某些类型、某些目标读者群就喜欢慢节奏的阅读体验；而另一些读者群则偏爱节奏明快的故事。同样，某些小说题材更适合短促、洗练的快节奏，而这种节奏通过使用更接地气、更简单的词汇更容易实现。因此，请留心你的用词复杂度对读者消化文本速度所产生的直接影响。

我们回头看看《我们得谈谈凯文》里的片段。这就是一个通过高级词汇成功塑造小说人物的极佳案例。但在我看来，这本书最适合慢读，而那些高级的词汇恰好能让读者慢下来，与主角并肩去真正经历那个故事。在这段话里，女主角正在描述她那个让人头疼的儿子凯文和女儿西莉亚在婴儿时期的区别：

“凯文那令人愁苦的婴儿期已经演变成了彻头彻尾的厌烦。而西莉亚则会被最微不足道的小玩意儿迷住，哪怕是一张彩色的薄棉纸，也照样能逗得她和摇篮上挂着的昂贵珍珠母音乐风铃一样开心。她对这个触觉世界展现出了一种不加选择的迷恋，这足以让麦迪逊大道的广告大师们抓狂。”

我们可以将它与另一本书对比一下。同样在我看来，这本书读起来要轻快得多，但字里行间的个性和人物塑造却一点不少。这是恰克·帕拉尼克的小说《幸存者（Survivor）》中的一个片段。从这个例子里我们可以清醒地看到，简单的词汇并不意味着你写不出深刻的见解或独特的风格：

“免提电话旁放着一本厚厚的日志本，里面塞满了他们要我完成的任务。他们要我按照他们的方式，一件一件，为接下来的十年负责。你生活中的一切都变成了一个个清单项目，变成了需要去完成的任务。你开始看到自己的生活被拍扁后的样子。两点之间最短的距离是一个时间轴，一个日程表，一张属于你时间的地图，一份余生的行程单。没有什么能像一张清单那样，把从现在通往死亡的直线看得清清楚楚。”

《幸存者》的这段话基本只有小学三年级的阅读难度，而《我们得谈谈凯文》的那段话则达到了初中三年级的水平。这在阅读门槛上是个巨大的坎，但两本书给人的感觉都非常成熟、有趣，且人物特色鲜明。所以，用浅显的文字写作绝对不等同于“低级”或“不成熟”。它改变的只是阅读的物理速度。

我记得当时读《幸存者》大概只花了倒腾两三天时间，而读《我们得谈谈凯文》可能前后花了一个半月。在我的体验中，后者就是一本需要你细细咀嚼的小说。好好琢磨你挑选的词汇，以及它们如何影响着小说的节奏。根据你的故事本身的诉求来调整——你想让它被多快读完？它应该以怎样的速度被阅读？词汇的深浅，会成为指引读者停留多久的隐形路标。

11. 避免使用“启动式动作”

这也是一个超级超级容易修改的细节。总是让角色“开始（start to）”或“着手（begin to）”做某个动作，会大大削弱动作的即时感，而且几乎对读者的理解没有任何帮助。

例如：“伊丽莎白开始笑了起来（Elizabeth started to laugh）” 和 “伊丽莎白笑了（Elizabeth laughed）”。在绝大多数情况下，它们传达的意思完全一样，但“伊丽莎白笑了”听起来要利落、直接得多。同理，“马克冲上前去，一拳打在约翰脸上（Mark lunged forward and punched John）”，绝对比 “马克冲上前去，开始殴打约翰（…and began to punch John）” 更有冲击力。同样，如果用“开始”确实会改变句子原本的意思，那你就保留它；但在九成情况下，这只是作者下意识笔误养成的坏习惯，把“开始”和“着手”从你的动作描写里无情删掉，你的文字会瞬间变得扎实有力。

12. 删掉废话和冗余词汇

当然，啰唆（Wordiness）有时可以是一种刻意的风格选择，但我这里说的不是文体风格上的繁复，而是指纯粹使用了大量对传达意思毫无帮助的废话。这会拉开读者与画面的距离，把你想表达的核心论点埋没在字里行间，让你的文字显得臃肿不堪，失去原本应有的干练与清爽。

我们来看这个例子，它非常典型地展现了很多人“用力过猛”的原因——那就是没完没了地用“……的（of）”来解释一件事：

“查尔斯从树的树桩上拿起了斧头，握紧了斧头的柄，把鞋的后跟踩进了他身下的泥土里。”

这句话完全可以精简为：

“查尔斯从树桩上拿起斧头，握紧木柄，把后跟踩进泥土里。”

我们不需要写“树的树桩”，因为大家都知道树桩属于树；不需要写“鞋的后跟”，因为我们默认查尔斯穿着鞋；也不需要写“斧头的柄”，因为前面刚说了他拿的是斧头。

除了滥用“……的”是典型的废话标志外，“他身下的泥土”也是多余的——泥土本来就在他脚底下。同样，一些没有必要的方向或位置描写也会让文字变得极其臃肿。例如，如果你写：“查尔斯抬头看着他头顶上的天空。” 我们早就知道天空在头顶上，所以“头顶上”就是纯粹的废话。

单独来看，这一两个词可能算不上什么天大的问题。但在整部小说的体量下，这些累赘会滚雪球般变成几万字的废话，让整个故事变得拖沓而充满塑料感。不过请记住，有时候为了调整句子的韵律、节奏，或者为了强调某个必须交代的细节，多写几个字是合理的。比如在刚才的例子里，如果你想特别强调查尔斯穿的鞋，你可以写：“他把靴子的后跟（the heel of his boot）踩进土里”，因为你想让读者明确知道他穿的是一双硬质靴子，而不是普通的布鞋。在有些时候，为了目的明确，你可以保留这些词，但绝大多数时候，这都是需要被无情修剪的枝桠。
