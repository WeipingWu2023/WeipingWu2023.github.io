In 2026, millions of computer users still use Windows 10, even though Windows 11 has been available for more than three years.

Today, there are more than 1.3 billion devices running Windows 10 worldwide on a monthly basis. But this is because several users began to notice that their system no longer felt like their own, but rather like a showcase full of advertisements and unsolicited notifications.

Microsoft has basically been systematically exerting dominance over its users, to the point that you question whether your machine is yours, or if it is Microsoft’s and you’re just paying for it.

It is estimated that more than 46% of devices worldwide were left without official support for Windows 11 because they required a new chip that many computers did not have, with Microsoft consigning millions of PCs to the ash heaps of history well before their time, all because of new TPM and security requirements.

At the same time, the system began to display recommendations and promotions even on devices where a full license had been purchased. These little pop-ups will appear for local account users and will mention all the free services you can get if you log in with a Microsoft account.

In turn, Microsoft allocated millions of dollars to its artificial intelligence partnership, prioritizing the increase of its market value rather than focusing on the user.

The tech behemoth, the world’s second most valuable company, says it plans to invest $30 billion into UK artificial intelligence infrastructure by 2028.

The problems do not stop, and more and more users feel trapped in a system that disappoints them.

So why is Windows 11 failing so horribly wrong?

For decades, Windows was considered the dominant operating system in the world of personal computers. For example, in 2015, it reached nearly 90% market share in desktop computers, which made it the almost undisputed standard in homes, offices, and educational centers.

However, over the years, Microsoft’s strategy changed profoundly, shifting from selling products to prioritizing services and subscriptions.

In 2023, its cloud service revenue exceeded $110 billion and represented nearly a quarter of the company.

“Azure cloud growth again, another beat here — 40% growth year-over-year compared to expectations of 38.2%.”

This change not only modified the business model, but also the everyday experience of the home user.

Windows 11 stopped being perceived solely as software installed on a device. It became a platform connected to external services, with online accounts and the cloud as its central access.

According to recent reports, more than 70% of Microsoft’s revenue comes from services, cloud, and subscriptions — not from traditional licenses.

What was once a relatively neutral tool began to integrate into an ecosystem designed to generate recurring revenue and collect usage data.

This opened the debate about whether the system still prioritizes privacy and performance, or if it now puts profitability first.

These changes have made a lot of people wonder: Is Windows still a tool for the user, or is it a cash extraction exercise for Microsoft?

One of the first symptoms of this transformation was the shift toward recurring revenue through subscriptions.

At the beginning of the last decade, more than 50% of the company’s revenue came directly from traditional software licenses. This led to the proportion tilting toward connected digital services 10 years later.

Moreover, the increasing requirement to use an online account is another central point of controversy.

In recent versions, the installation process almost forces users to sign in with an online account, making the local account option less visible.

In certain markets, 100% of standard configurations guide users first toward online authentication before allowing other alternatives.

This results in each session being linked to external services, which facilitates synchronization but also expands data collection.

It is estimated that more than 1 billion devices are linked to accounts within the company’s ecosystem, turning each use into a potential digital record.

“Because you’re not just using Windows — Windows is using you.”

But this isn’t the first channel to tell you that Windows is stealing your data, and it definitely won’t be the last.

Everything you do on Windows is being tracked.

Another issue that emerged among users was the implementation of the TPM chip, which marked a turning point in public perception.

Also called the Trusted Platform Module, this chip serves to improve device security by protecting keys, passwords, and sensitive data at the hardware level.

It comes integrated in most computers manufactured approximately from 2016 onward.

However, although it was said to be for security reasons, in practice it left about 240 million computers without support, even though they were still functioning properly.

Many of these devices could run office tasks, browsing, and studying without issues.

After the announcement of the new requirements, the global personal computer market recorded sales increases of nearly 15% in the following period.

For millions of people, especially in less-resourced regions, it meant choosing between buying a new device or staying without updates and assuming greater risks.

“It will tell you this computer can’t run Windows 11, and even though it doesn’t exactly tell you why, a lot of people are confused about this. For the most part, it’s down to not having one of these TPM modules.”

Another important point is that Microsoft began to strongly invest in AI.

The company allocated around $13 billion to its partnership with OpenAI, placing artificial intelligence at the center of its digital ecosystem.

As a result, traditional tools began incorporating automated assistance, text generation, and predictive suggestions across multiple applications.

However, in recent studies, five out of 10 users indicated that they do not regularly use these advanced features.

Even so, the constant AI buttons and notifications changed the visual experience and system functionality, disrupting user interaction.

“As we lost the functionality and the clean UI, in exchange we got useless news and weather tabs, along with mandatory AI.”

Another of the most controversial cases was the Recall feature, created to periodically capture on-screen activity and facilitate later searches.

Depending on daily use, this tool can generate thousands of screenshots in a single day, creating an extremely detailed historical archive.

Cybersecurity experts warn that databases of such magnitude constitute attractive targets for cyberattacks.

For example, recent global reports show that more than 70% of serious incidents involve local information that ends up being exploited by malware.

This has led users to not feel completely protected, since this could lead to a failure that might expose personal data.

Security researchers later found that the way Recall stored data was not fully protected and could be accessed by malware or anyone with local access.

Users no longer feel secure with the Windows 11 system, and this has led to a gradual migration toward alternative systems.

Although Windows remains dominant, Linux-based distributions represent around 2% of the global desktop market. This equals millions of active devices.

Additionally, after changes in hardware requirements, some communities saw up to 30% more downloads of alternative systems.

For example, macOS maintains around 8% market share in developed markets, being an option for those seeking closed integration and fewer ads.

While these figures do not represent a massive abandonment, they do show a progressive shift of advanced users seeking greater control.

In 2020, Windows had almost 83% market share by unit shipments, while ChromeOS had 10% and macOS had 7%.

The evolution of Windows 11 reflects a tension between technological innovation and a business model oriented toward data and services.

More than 1.4 billion devices worldwide still use some version of Windows, making each strategic decision a global-scale phenomenon.

However, in recent surveys, four out of 10 users said they are concerned about the information collected by their modern operating systems.

This shows that privacy has become a central issue in the digital debate.

Windows has always collected some information in the background, but over the years, that quiet data collection grew into something that people could no longer ignore.

The Windows 11 crisis is not only technical or aesthetic, but also a transformation in the relationship between user and platform.

If the focus continues to be monetization and data collection, public perception could worsen over time.

On the other hand, if local performance, transparency, and real choice are prioritized again, the system could regain part of the lost trust — the trust that made Windows the almost absolute standard of personal computing for decades.
