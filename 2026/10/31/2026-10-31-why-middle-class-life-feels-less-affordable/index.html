Why Does Middle-Class Life Feel Less Affordable?

LA is just like, I don't even know how people live here. It's insane. It is impossible to live in Ontario. Everything is so expensive. London, I don't know what type of limited edition Oliver Twist lifestyle you're selling to us, but we can't hack it. It's too much.

In a recent survey, between 75% and 91% of Europeans reported that prices in shops are rising faster than their incomes. And in America, the number is a whopping 90%.

So middle class life is clearly getting less and less affordable. But why?

The most popular explanations emphasize rising inequality, the skyrocketing cost of housing, college and health care, as well as rapidly rising energy and food prices after the pandemic. And yeah, this all sounded pretty plausible.

But when we dug into the data, we ran into a pretty serious problem with the entire idea that middle class life is getting less and less affordable. Sure, most people report it, but on paper they are wrong, both in the US and Europe.

Median wages, adjusted for inflation, are up 20 to 30% compared to the year 2000. And remember, that's the median wage, not the average. So a handful of billionaires are not ruling the trend here.

Sure, some stuff has clearly gotten much more expensive, like college childcare and those kind of things. But because what used to be true middle class luxuries like a car, clothing, toys for kids, or a massive TV, these have all gotten so much cheaper that it kind of balances out on paper.

So what's going on? Why do people feel like they are living in a time of stagflation? That is, the cost of living rising faster than income. While the data says we are actually in a pretty healthy economy, there are really only two possible answers.

Either people are missing something and being irrationally pessimistic, or the official data is missing something.

But at the same time, I do know that the way that inflation is calculated has changed over time and that it's far from perfect.

For example, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the institution in charge of measuring U.S. inflation, claims that housing prices have increased below average wages. And come on, that that can't be right. Anyone who has recently tried to buy a home knows that this cannot be true.

So without further ado, let's start with the first theory: the theory of the vibe session.

Because while inflation has undeniably been higher than we were used to for the longest time, unemployment honestly looks really good compared to historical standards on both sides of the Atlantic.

And that's very important, because while we were all focusing on rapidly rising prices, driven especially by energy and food, something remarkable was happening in the labor market right after Covid, thanks to big government stimulus during lockdowns and then everyone going outside to spend all of that money.

Unemployment fell dramatically and median wages jumped up massively. Sure, it's true that inflation hit hard in 2022, and that almost entirely eroded this impressive wage growth. But if we look at the graph, median wages have been growing ever since, even faster than inflation.

So then why was consumer sentiment collapsing?

Some economists pointed to the fact that during the post-Covid period, media framing of economic news became increasingly sensationalist. Negative.

But in a recent massive survey, Harvard economist Stephanie Stern Java uncovered a deeper psychological bias in how human brains process money.

You see, we experience pay rises and price hikes completely differently. Inflation usually raises both wages and prices at the same time. But when people get a raise, they tend to credit their own hard work or talent.

Well, if prices in supermarkets soar, they do not connect that these two things together. They do not see them as part of the same high pressure economy. Instead, inflation feels like an arbitrary pay cut forced upon you by greedy corporations or inept politicians.

So while few linked the Great resignation of 2021 directly to inflation, the 2022 price surge was widely felt and largely blamed on bad politicians and greedy corporations, and this frustration could explain why consumer sentiment dropped so sharply, even though on paper wages had gone up faster.

Baumol's cost disease

Let's deep dive back into our second theory for why middle class life feels increasingly unaffordable: Bombs cost disease.

Okay, let's go back to 1993. I was but a mere child. And yeah, I do remember that according to my middle class parents who were teachers, goods were considered pretty expensive.

Whenever our washing machine broke, my parents, I remember, had to save months to replace it in big television with something that I, as a child of course, really wanted, but my parents told me they were outrageously expensive.

Also toys for kids.

But now, as an adult with an income similar to that, what my parents had honestly, toys for my kids. They just feel really cheap, especially compared to our super high monthly daycare bill.

And actually, if you look at this graph, we can see that it's not just me imagining things. Inflation has not been just all prices going up. At the same time, the prices of services like college tuition and childcare have gone up by a lot, while simultaneously prices of goods like clothing, toys and TVs have gone down significantly in comparison.

Already in a famous 1960s paper, economist William Barmore theorized that this is no coincidence. These are two sides of the same coin.

The logic is the following.

Imagine that the economy only has teachers and farmers. Thanks to the invention of tractors and fertilizers, each farm is now able to produce and sell much more wheat and earn more money.

However, the teacher cannot use the same technologies to teach more things to more students. One teacher, one classroom, 30 kids, just like a century ago.

But there's a problem. If farmers are suddenly earning four times more because technology made them four times more productive, why would anyone stay teacher?

To prevent a total collapse of the education system, schools have to raise the wages of teachers to keep up with those of farmers. Essentially, we are raising the weight of teachers not because they're more coming, more productive, but because productivity is growing in other sectors.

And as a consequence, the relative cost of educating each child quadruples, while the cost of each loaf of bread limits.

Now, of course, it's important to stress nobody in this economy is poorer. They are all richer. The farmer can make a lot more bread, and because his income rose as much as the teachers, he can still afford the same education for his kids as before.

The teachers, whose pay track the farmers, can afford also those classes for their kids and they can afford much more bread.

So what has changed is not just how rich they both are, but much more so, what things cost relative to each other. Bread now feels almost free, but well, we then might have expected that schools would get cheaper. To the opposite actually happens: schools now feel outrageously expensive.

That is, bombs cost disease, TV production, toy production, clothing production. It all has become much more efficient. But college child care and medical care, they all still operate roughly similarly to how they did in 1993.

So according to this theory, the graph is exactly what we would have expected to happen. Wages had to rise in universities, daycare centers and hospitals just to keep up with rising wages elsewhere. And because these services did not get more efficient, their price had to go up a lot as wages went up.

At the same time, interestingly, because many of these services in Europe are done by the government, cause disease can also help explain why our taxes in Europe have gone up and up.

Well, it doesn't feel like government services have gotten much better. Government costs have simply gotten higher and higher compared to the economy, as they had to keep raising wages in low productivity sectors to keep up with a much more productive private sector.

Indeed, as you can see in this graph from Norway, this is exactly what happened. Private sector prices went up far slower than those in the government, and defense, which relies heavily on machines, was somewhere in between.

But what does the changing cost structure of services versus goods have to do with a feeling that middle class life keeps getting more expensive?

Well, let's go back to the life of my parents in the 1990s. They worried about being able to afford the latest computer that I might need for school, or that they might need for their work. They worried about not having enough savings to replace a broken washing machine or a broken car, but their mortgage, that was pretty low.

Their local taxes were pretty low, childcare was pretty cheap, and so was my education.

Meanwhile, what worries me is my mortgage bill, a massive daycare bill. And since I live in Europe, the rising tax burden which of course indirectly funds my kid's education.

Yet honestly, I was just ordering a massive TV the other day for my channel and I was surprised how cheap it was. Same for these super fancy toys for my kids. The cost of these toys is just almost nothing compared to the monthly daycare bill.

So what if the reason that people feel like the middle class life is getting more fragile is that what used to be expensive were essentially luxury goods? Well, what is expensive today are essentials.

That is, if one of my parents lost their income, they may have needed to buy a used washing machine rather than a new one. They may have had to work on an old computer longer, and we could not have the fancy TV the neighbors just got.

But when I lose my income, I am at risk of losing my house. I'm at risk of not being able to afford daycare, making finding a new job even more difficult.

So is by most cost disease, simply making middle class life feeling more fragile? I think this is part of the explanation, absolutely.

But when I was looking at the graph, something strange stood out to me, because I'm very sure my mortgage bill today is much larger compared to my income than that of my parents back in the 1990s. But according to this graph, that's not the case.

So I still think we need our third theory as well.

Inflation statistics and housing costs

There is something wrong with inflation statistics. Why do official statistics say that housing costs went up more slowly than wages did? While that's clearly not the case, when we looked at the way inflation is calculated, we essentially found four reasons why rising house costs do not properly show up in inflation statistics.

The first is that because a home is both seen as something that people live in and an investment, economists do not just look at house prices directly.

After all, if I own a home with no mortgage and that house gets worth more, it, doesn't that feel like inflation to me? It feels like I'm getting richer.

Therefore, in the US, economists instead calculate true housing inflation by looking at how much homeowners could have rented out their place for.

But because house prices have gone up much faster than rents did since 2000, this completely misses that people who recently bought a home have a far higher monthly mortgage payment now than what they used to have.

And to make matters worse, since many renters are actually in rent controlled units where rent barely changes every year, average rent increases look much lower than they actually are for a young person or family signing a brand new lease today.

And third, the official rent increase number is a nationwide average. But we all know that the real housing crisis is concentrated in growing economic hubs where the good jobs actually are.

The cheap, stagnant rent in Toledo, Ohio or Hala Germany artificially offsets an expensive surging rent in New York or Frankfurt.

And finally, what's especially relevant after Covid, official inflation statistics do not take into account the costs of borrowing.

That is, until 1983, U.S. inflation used include mortgage interest rates. In Europe, they never counted this at all.

All of this made little difference, though, when rates were falling or stayed near zero for an entire decade. But when interest rates started rising rapidly, inflation statistics suddenly missed a major part of people's expenses.

How much?

Well, a 2024 paper by Boaz Gummer Schultz and Larry Summers rebuilt inflation the old way with mortgage, car loan and credit card interest rates included. And by that measure, US inflation in late 2022 was not 9%, which is very high. It was actually 18% instead.

And to me, this closes the rest of the puzzle.

After the Covid lockdowns, median wages increased a lot, even above the official inflation numbers. But once you add borrowing costs back to inflation, it makes sense that anyone with a car loan, credit card debt, or simply trying to buy an apartment with a mortgage genuinely felt like their wage was not keeping up with their costs.

They were right.

Specifically, the authors estimate that the cost of borrowing explains roughly three quarters of the gap between how bad people say things are and how good the statistics look.

Conclusion

So in conclusion, why does middle class life keep getting more expensive? Or is it actually getting more expensive?

To me, I think the best answer comes from the last two theories. Baumol's cost disease theory can explain why a lot of middle class life staples have gotten a lot more expensive, and how that makes middle class life feel much more fragile than it used to.

It is due to increased productivity in goods that were essentially luxuries. The cost of many essential services have skyrocketed in advanced economies, and since it was much easier to give up buying a big TV than canceling your mortgage, the result is that middle class life feels a lot more fragile today than it did decades ago.

That being said, while on paper the median wages have kept up with inflation, we have seen that inflation still feels really bad.

And more importantly, in my opinion, if you are not in a rent controlled housing unit, or if you did not own a house already for years and years, then inflation was genuinely quite a lot higher than the official statistics suggest.

先看懂结论

这支视频不是说「所有人都变穷了」。它的重点是：平均统计数字与家庭真正感受到的压力可以同时存在。

  • 工资扣除通胀后，长期看可能确实上涨；电视、衣服、玩具等可工业化生产的商品也更便宜。
  • 但住房、托育、教育、医疗等「很难靠机器大幅提高效率」的服务更贵，而且它们是很难不买的生活必需项。
  • 官方通胀指标也未必充分呈现新买房者、刚搬家租房者、或需要借贷者面对的成本。

因此，「账面上可负担」不等于「生活中有安全感」。当一份收入突然中断时，少买一台电视通常没事；但付不起房贷、托育或医疗费，会马上冲击生活的基本结构。

三条解释线

1. Vibecession：感受衰退

疫情后，工资和就业曾改善，但物价上涨更容易被人直接感受到。人们会把加薪归因于自己的努力，却把超市涨价视为外部带来的「减薪」。媒体的负面叙事会放大这种感觉。这个解释能说明短期情绪，却不能解释几十年来的压力。

2. Baumol’s cost disease：鲍莫尔成本病

工厂和农业可以借助技术不断提高每名员工的产出；教师、护士、幼儿园老师却不能把一小时变成两小时。为了留住这些服务业员工，工资仍要跟着其他行业上升，于是教育、医疗、托育等服务的价格相对越来越高。这不代表服务人员「效率低」，而是服务的质量本来就需要人和时间。

3. 通胀统计漏掉了谁的压力？

视频提出：住房在统计中常用「业主等价租金」而非房价衡量；全国平均租金也可能掩盖就业中心城市的新租约压力。传统 CPI 还未充分反映利率上升时房贷、车贷和信用卡利息对借款人的冲击。注意：这是视频的分析框架，不应把某一项估计直接当作对所有国家、所有家庭都成立的事实。

高频词与表达

  • affordable：负担得起的。Housing is no longer affordable for many young families.
  • median wage：工资中位数；不是平均工资。它表示处在正中间那个人的工资，更不容易被极高收入者拉高。
  • adjusted for inflation：按通胀调整后，即看「实际购买力」而非票面金额。
  • price hike：价格猛涨。Consumers are frustrated by repeated price hikes.
  • stagflation：滞胀，通常指经济增长疲软、物价却上涨的困难组合。
  • productivity：生产率，单位时间或单位劳动能产出多少东西。
  • relative cost：相对成本。不是单看价格，而是看它与收入、其他商品相比的负担。
  • rent-controlled：受租金管制的；已有租约可能涨得很慢，但新租客的价格未必如此。
  • mortgage：房贷；mortgage payment 是每月还款额。
  • keep up with：跟上、赶得上。My salary is not keeping up with living costs.

你该带走的知识

  1. 不要只看一个指标。工资、CPI、房价、租金、利率和家庭债务反映的是不同切面。
  2. 区分存量与增量。早已买房且固定利率的人，与刚买房、续租、需要新贷款的人，受到同一轮通胀和加息的冲击可能完全不同。
  3. 区分平均数与「不可替代的必需品」。商品降价未必能抵消住房、医疗、托育等刚性支出的压力。
  4. 学习一句最实用的英文总结：On paper, incomes may be rising; in daily life, essentials can still feel out of reach.
    账面上收入可能在增长；但日常生活中，必需品仍可能让人觉得遥不可及。
